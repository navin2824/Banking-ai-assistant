FINANCIAL RISK MANAGEMENT — CONCEPTS FOR DOMAIN QUESTION ANSWERING
Document type: Educational domain reference Scope: Banking and
financial-system risk concepts Prepared: 11 November 2026

PURPOSE AND SCOPE This document describes common financial risks and
principles for evaluating AI answers in a banking context. It is
intended as educational domain material and a starting point for model
testing. It does not provide investment recommendations, credit
decisions or institution-specific regulatory limits.

1.  CREDIT RISK Credit risk is the possibility that a borrower or
    counterparty will fail to meet contractual obligations. Common
    assessment factors include repayment history, income and cash-flow
    capacity, leverage, collateral, industry conditions and
    concentration. Credit risk can arise from loans, bonds, derivatives,
    settlement exposures and other counterparty relationships. A credit
    decision should not be based on one indicator alone. A credit score
    may be useful, but it does not replace affordability analysis,
    verification and applicable underwriting policy.

2.  LIQUIDITY RISK Liquidity risk is the risk that an institution cannot
    meet cash-flow obligations when due without unacceptable losses. A
    bank may be solvent but still face liquidity pressure if it cannot
    obtain cash quickly enough to meet withdrawals or payments. Banks
    manage liquidity through cash and liquid-asset buffers, funding
    diversification, maturity monitoring, contingency funding plans and
    stress testing. Liquidity risk should be assessed across different
    time horizons and under both normal and stressed conditions.

3.  MARKET RISK Market risk is the possibility of loss from movements in
    market prices or rates, including interest rates, foreign exchange
    rates, equity prices and commodity prices. Exposure depends on
    positions, sensitivities, hedges and market conditions. A change in
    market rates can affect the value of assets and liabilities
    differently. A risk report should state the relevant portfolio,
    valuation date, assumptions and measurement method.

4.  OPERATIONAL RISK Operational risk arises from inadequate or failed
    processes, people, systems or external events. Examples include
    processing errors, cyber incidents, fraud, third-party failures,
    internal misconduct and business disruption. Controls include
    segregation of duties, access management, reconciliation, staff
    training, incident response, audit trails and business continuity
    planning. Controls reduce exposure but cannot guarantee that
    incidents will never occur.

5.  CONCENTRATION RISK Concentration risk occurs when exposures are
    heavily concentrated in a borrower, connected group, sector,
    geography, funding source or asset class. A concentrated portfolio
    can be vulnerable to a common adverse event. Diversification and
    exposure limits can reduce, but do not eliminate, this risk.
    Concentration should be examined by relevant dimensions and with
    clear definitions of exposure.

6.  CAPITAL ADEQUACY Capital provides a loss-absorbing buffer. Capital
    adequacy frameworks compare eligible capital with risk-weighted
    exposures or other prescribed measures. Capital definitions,
    deductions, buffers and minimum ratios are regulatory matters and
    must be checked against the current rule applicable to the
    institution. A model should not quote an exact regulatory capital
    minimum without identifying the entity type, applicable framework
    and effective date.

7.  STRESS TESTING Stress testing evaluates how a portfolio or
    institution might perform under severe but plausible adverse
    scenarios. Scenarios may include recession, rising defaults, falling
    asset prices, interest-rate shocks, deposit outflows or
    foreign-exchange movements. Results depend on assumptions, data
    quality, model design and management actions. Stress-test results
    are not forecasts and should not be presented as predictions of what
    will certainly happen. Sensitivity analysis can help show which
    assumptions have the largest effect.

8.  RISK GOVERNANCE Effective risk governance includes clear
    accountability, independent oversight, documented policies, reliable
    data, escalation processes and regular review. Risk limits should
    have owners and procedures for handling breaches. Senior management
    and boards need information that is timely, accurate and
    understandable. A model-generated risk summary should preserve
    uncertainty and should not replace accountable human review.

9.  MODEL RISK AND AI RELIABILITY Model risk is the possibility that
    decisions or outcomes are adversely affected by errors in model
    design, assumptions, data, implementation or use. A financial-domain
    AI system can introduce additional risks if it invents facts,
    misquotes a regulation, confuses products or presents uncertain
    output as certain. Reliability work should test representative
    questions, edge cases, ambiguous wording, outdated information and
    questions that cannot be answered from the supplied documents.

10. PRINCIPLES FOR RELIABLE FINANCIAL AI

-   Separate sourced facts from calculations, assumptions and opinions.
-   Cite the document, section and date supporting material claims.
-   Do not invent regulatory thresholds, rates, financial results or
    citations.
-   State when information is missing, outdated or conflicting.
-   Ask for the institution type, product, jurisdiction and as-of date
    when these affect the answer.
-   For numerical calculations, show inputs, formula, units and
    rounding.
-   Avoid presenting general information as personalised investment,
    lending or legal advice.
-   Escalate high-impact or customer-specific decisions to qualified
    human review.
